Summer begins with a solstice. In the Southern Hemisphere, it takes place around December 21 and marks the longest day and the shortest night of the year. At this moment, the Sun reaches its highest apparent point in the sky, and the light lingers longer illuminating the landscape.

Litha celebrates exactly this instant.

If Yule marks the gradual return of light, Litha represents its peak. It is the season of abundance, of the ripening of fruits, of the energy that seems to overflow from nature.

Litha is a major sabbat, celebrated in summer.

In contemporary pagan traditions, the summer solstice symbolizes the peak of the solar cycle. It is a moment to celebrate the vitality of the earth, the harvests that begin to ripen, and the strength of life in its most intense expression.

Precisely when the Sun reaches its highest point, the path back also begins. After the solstice, the days slowly begin to shorten. The change is hardly noticed in daily life, but it has already begun.

Nature shows us that growth and decline are not opposites. They are parts of the same movement. The fruit ripens because there was spring. And ripening means, inevitably, preparing the next cycle.

In a world that seems to demand constant productivity, permanent success, and infinite growth, summer reminds us that not even the Sun remains forever at its highest point.

In the Southern Hemisphere, Litha takes place in the middle of December, when the Brazilian summer begins.

It is a time of intense heat, late-afternoon rains, mango trees laden with fruit, cashews, pequi, late jabuticabas in some regions, and an explosion of life that occupies backyards, gardens, and forests.

Brazilian Indigenous peoples also organize many of their calendars by the cycles of nature, observing rains, fruitings, and animal behavior. Although these traditions have no historical connection to Litha, they share the same attention to the rhythms of the earth and to the moment of abundance.

Celebrating Litha in Brazil can simply mean recognizing this force of the season and realizing that it is also part of our landscape.

My most curious memory of summer is a little contradictory.

It was the time when I argued the most with school vacations. The neighborhood children would run after kites early in the morning, shouting beneath my bedroom window and interrupting any attempt to sleep in. A true assault on rest.

But summer also holds one of my most beautiful memories.

I remember the old daylight saving time, when the sun took longer to set. I would go down to the backyard and spend hours in a huge garden of cosmos flowers that my mother cultivated. Butterflies would come to visit the flowers, and I would watch that delicate movement until the sun set.

When I found a butterfly that had already died, I used to keep it between the pages of a book. The other day I found one of them. It was still there, silent, preserving a small memory of that summer. I think I was overcome by nostalgia.

And remember: among some modern druidic traditions, the summer solstice is also known as **Alban Hefin**, an expression traditionally translated as “Light of Summer.” The name celebrates the moment when light reaches its peak, reminding us that even the longest day of the year is part of a cycle that keeps turning.
