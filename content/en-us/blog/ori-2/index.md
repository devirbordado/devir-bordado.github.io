Have you ever stopped to think that, within you, there is a divine spark that holds your essence, your destiny, and your connection to the sacred? In traditions of African origin, this spark has a name: Ori. But Ori is much more than a religious concept—it dialogues profoundly with psychology, especially with the idea of self-knowledge and personal transformation.

Ori is a word from the Yoruba language that literally means “head.” But don’t be mistaken—we are not speaking only of the physical organ. Ori is your spiritual head, a divine spark that exists within every human being.

In the tradition of Umbanda and Candomblé, Ori is understood in two complementary ways: as an Orisha within the African pantheon, where it is considered the most important of the orishas, for it is the personal divinity that accompanies each individual from birth to death. A Yoruba proverb summarizes this importance: “There is no orisha that supports a person more than their own Ori.” But Ori is also understood as Inner Consciousness. It is your essence, your personality, your “inner self.” It is the force that determines who you are, your behavior patterns, and your life purpose.

Caring for Ori means cultivating positive thoughts, good attitudes, and elevated character.

Ori dialogues directly with depth psychology, especially with the concepts of Carl Gustav Jung. The Swiss psychologist developed the idea of the Self, which represents the totality of the psyche, the center of the personality that integrates conscious and unconscious.

The connection between Ori and the Self is profound. For both the vision of Ori and for Jung, life is a journey of self-discovery. The process of individuation, for Jung, is the path of becoming who you truly are, integrating all parts of your personality. In Ifá, this journey is guided by Ori itself, which contains your destiny and your essence.

In analytical psychology, archetypes are universal patterns of the collective unconscious. In this sense, Ori can be understood as the archetype of the individual Self, the image that represents the totality and the center of each person’s personality.

Caring for Ori is, therefore, nurturing the process of individuation, seeking to become a more complete and fulfilled person.

Here we arrive at one of the most beautiful and profound aspects of this connection, which is the role of Ori in the processes of symbolic death and rebirth.

In the Ifá tradition, the Odu Oyeku Meji is the ruler of endings and profound transformations. It does not speak of physical death, but of something that needs to die within you—a habit, a fear, a relationship, an old version of yourself. This “death” is the door to rebirth, a return to the cosmic womb to prepare for a new way of existing.

In Jungian psychology, the process of individuation often begins with a crisis, a “wound in the personality” that takes the ego off its axis. This moment requires the person to bid farewell to their old identifications and face their hidden parts (the shadow) so that a new totality can emerge.

In summary, Ori is not a mere observer in moments of transformation—it is the core of the process, acting as the spiritual center that guides, the essence that must be rescued, and the symbol of the totality to be achieved.

By establishing this dialogue with Jungian psychology, we realize that African ancestral wisdom has understood, for millennia, something that modern psychology only began to map in the 20th century about the journey of self-knowledge and the search for inner totality.
