Revisiting my memory of the exhibition *Avesso do avesso*(Reverse of the Reverse), by Julian Campos, held at the Ribeirão Preto Museum of Art in 2024, some thoughts led me to this text.

If there is a question that every person who embroiders has already heard, it is this:

“Can I see the back?”

It’s curious.

Almost no one asks how long the embroidery took to finish. Almost no one asks why that design was chosen or how many times the thread had to be undone.

They want to see the back.

For a long time I thought this was just technical curiosity. Today I think differently. The back is the only place where the embroidery records the path.

On the front we see only the result. On the back, the decisions remain.

Every color change. Every interrupted stitch. Every thread that had to go back a few centimeters to find the right place. Every change of direction.

It is there that the process continues to exist.

Did you know that documents also have a back?

When we find an old photograph, we see the portrait. But the back may hold a date written in pencil, a dedication, a signature.

Sometimes, just a piece of adhesive tape indicates that the image once occupied another place before reaching us.

The same happens with objects.

A restored vase holds almost invisible cracks. An old book carries stains left by the fingers that leafed through it for decades. A dress preserves small deformations produced by the body that wore it.

These are records that rarely appear in what we call the “work.”

But they remain inscribed in the material. With embroidery it is no different.

There are those who consider a perfect back to be one in which there are almost no visible threads. It is an admirable demonstration of technique.

But when I teach someone to embroider, I explain that this is not the only possible back. I like the ones in which it is still possible to recognize the path of the thread.

Because the thread thinks. The thread records decisions. And every decision leaves a trace.

And, precisely for that reason, it reveals its own learning.

In astrology there is an archetype called Pallas, associated with the intelligence that organizes patterns. It is not only about logic, but about the ability to perceive hidden structures, to recognize order where, at first glance, only chaos seems to exist.

The back of embroidery produces exactly this effect. Someone who has never embroidered sees only crossed threads.

Someone who knows the craft can reconstruct the path taken by the needle, understand where the thread entered, why it changed direction, and how that sequence of decisions made the image on the front possible.

When we look at a person, we see only the front. The profession. The titles. The photographs. The achievements. The back remains hidden.

We do not see the abandoned choices. The interrupted projects. The people who taught something before leaving. The losses. The new beginnings. Nor the number of times it was necessary to undo an entire piece in order to continue.

Even so, all of that sustains the image we see today.

In the conservation of cultural goods, something similar happens. Often, the back matters as much as the front.

A restorer observes the support. The seams. The old patches. The materials used in previous interventions. It is there that the object’s material history remains recorded.

I am also a restorer of paper-based documents, and I learned during my training that not always is what sustains an object on the surface. Sometimes, the most important information is hidden.

On the back. In the patch. In the almost imperceptible mark left by time.

That is why I do not separate embroidery from memory.

Both hold what the surface does not reveal. And I see in this the greatest beauty of the back. It does not exist to compete with the front. It exists to remind us that every beautiful image was built by a path that almost no one saw.

May we, in this new year, be ready to embroider new paths.
