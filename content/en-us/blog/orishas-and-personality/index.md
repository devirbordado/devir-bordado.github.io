We live in curious times. On social media, memes about the “children of each Orisha” spread with the same lightness with which people talk about communicative Geminis or organized Virgos. “A child of Iemanjá is like this,” “a child of Oxóssi is roasted,” “a child of Nanã is grumpy.” There is, in fact, a tradition that associates certain personality traits with the Orishas. But these descriptions make more sense when they are understood more as symbols than as labels.

An interesting way of looking at this is through Analytical Psychology. Carl Jung described archetypes as universal images present in the collective unconscious symbolic patterns that cross cultures and help organize our experience of the world. Jung never wrote specifically about the Orishas, but various researchers have established approximations between analytical psychology and Afro-Brazilian cosmologies, understanding these deities as symbolic expressions of different dimensions of human experience. This reading does not replace their religious meaning; it only offers a possibility of dialogue between tradition and psychology.

In Umbanda, Iemanjá is the Great Mother: the one who welcomes, protects, and cares. Many houses tend to associate with her children characteristics such as sensitivity, generosity, and a strong bond with family. At the same time, challenges are also spoken of, such as the difficulty of letting go, the tendency to want to protect excessively, or resentment when there is a breach of trust.

These descriptions have always made sense to me.

My front Orisha is Iemanjá, and for a long time I noticed how much I would cancel myself within relationships, trying to take care of the other before taking care of myself. It was a difficult lesson. Today I understand that self-knowledge also means recognizing what needs to be transformed. Integrating the shadow is perhaps precisely this: looking at our potential without ignoring our fragilities.

Of course, there is also the humorous side. Social media loves to joke about the “children of each Orisha.” The memes exaggerate characteristics, make people laugh, and help popularize the theme. It is only worth remembering that they are caricatures, not complete descriptions of a person. After all, if it depended on the memes, every child of Iemanjá would live involved in five different gossip circles at the same time.

My adjacent Orisha is Oxóssi, the hunter of a single arrow. Traditionally, his children are described as observant, discreet, curious, and profoundly linked to knowledge and nature. Among his challenges appear excessive introspection, difficulty in trusting, and a certain tendency toward isolation.

I also recognize these aspects in myself.

It was through the symbolic energy of Oxóssi that I learned to associate patience, observation, and respect for the timing of things. Even so, I often live with the anxiety of wanting everything to happen right away. In those moments, I remember that the hunter does not waste arrows. He waits for the right instant before acting.

In recent years, I have also come to perceive in myself characteristics traditionally associated with Ossaim. I do not know if this represents a deeper understanding of my own spiritual path, a specific phase of life, or simply an aspect of my personality that I have only now managed to recognize.

In Umbanda tradition, the children of Ossaim are often described as reserved, patient people capable of developing deep mastery over what they study or practice. These are characteristics that dialogue greatly with my way of working. I can remain for hours, days, and sometimes months concentrated on a single research project, moved by the pleasure of understanding and perfecting what I do.

My ancestry is also linked to Nanã.

Considered one of the oldest among the Orishas, Nanã often represents wisdom, memory, and time. Among the characteristics traditionally attributed to her children are serenity, dignity, and the ability to advise. In her challenging dimension, there is talk of the difficulty in abandoning what has already been built, resistance to change, and attachment to the past.

Do you see how these descriptions approach astrology? They do not function as a sentence, but as an invitation to self-knowledge. Knowing that I identify with aspects symbolically associated with Iemanjá helps me perceive when I am trying to take care of people too much. Recognizing in myself traits of Oxóssi reminds me of the importance of trusting more. Thinking of Nanã makes me reflect on the balance between preserving memory and continuing to walk.

No archetype exists only in its luminous dimension. Every potency also carries challenges, and it is precisely recognizing these tensions that allows growth.

That is where therapy comes in.

Knowing your archetypes, whether they are Orishas, astrological signs, or other symbolic systems, is only the beginning. The real work consists of integrating the shadow: understanding that the same force that makes us protective can make us controlling; that the same capacity to observe can turn into isolation; that wisdom can also harden when it refuses to change.

This approximation between Afro-Brazilian mythology and psychology also appears in academic research. A study developed at USP, for example, uses the itans (sacred stories) of Nanã and Obaluaê to reflect on the treatment of patients with borderline personality disorder, showing how these narratives can contribute to therapeutic processes and to the understanding of human experience.

The Orishas are not a ready-made recipe for explaining people. Saying that “every child of Iemanjá is like this” makes as much sense as stating that every Scorpio has exactly the same personality. Each individual organizes these influences in a singular way.

We are an encounter of memories, experiences, affections, choices, and cultural contexts.

During my undergraduate thesis, I wrote a sentence that still summarizes this idea very well: “Personality is Embroidered Becoming.”

I still like it because it reminds us that no one is born ready. Just like an embroidery, our personality is built stitch by stitch. It results from lived experiences, the relationships we establish, the people we meet along the way, and the choices we make in the face of each event.

That is why even twin siblings, raised in the same house and sharing so many circumstances, develop completely different ways of feeling and interpreting the world.

Perhaps that is the greatest contribution of archetypes. They do not exist to fit us into rigid categories, but to offer mirrors. When we look at them merely as entertainment, we laugh for a few moments. When we use them to reflect on ourselves, they cease to be labels and become paths of self-knowledge.
