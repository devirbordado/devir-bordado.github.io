She does not bow. She does not lower her head. She does not ask permission to exist.

This year Anitta arrived on the platforms with two albums that really stirred up some bubbles.

I kept thinking: why is a free woman always a problem?

This woman has had many names. In ancient Greece, they called her Hecate, the lady of the crossroads, who moved between worlds and knew what few dared to know. In Hebrew mythology, they called her Lilith, the first woman, created from the same clay as Adam, who refused submission and was banished from paradise for it. In Umbanda terreiros, they call her Pombagira, the entity who drinks, dances, laughs loudly, and does not apologize for her freedom.

What do all of them have in common? They were demonized. Demoted to the role of “dangerous,” “impure,” “witches.” Because a woman who does not bow threatens the order. She reminds us that the world can be different.

The Nigerian writer Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie said in an interview: “We feel very uncomfortable when women talk about how they are oppressed and excluded.”

And she is right. The discomfort is not without reason. It is the fear that, by speaking, the woman will awaken others.

And what does the woman who has awakened do? She creates.

Rozsika Parker, in the book *The Subversive Stitch*, shows something that I myself experience at the embroidery hoop: embroidery has a contradictory history. At the same time that it was used to “domesticate” women, confining them to the private space, it also became a tool of silent resistance. Parker says that embroidery “inculcated feminine subservience at the same time that it provided an immensely pleasurable source of creativity, forging bonds between women.”

It is in this crack that we enter.

The Brazilian artist Rosana Paulino embroiders the memory of Black pain and resistance in Brazil. Her needle is not a pastime. It is a scream. The Mexican Romero uses thread as one uses a voice: to say that she is here, that she will not be silenced, that embroidering is also doing politics.

I, who research embroidery as this space of supervised freedom, see that society thought it was confining us with needle and thread. It did not realize that, in the apparent silence of embroidery, we were weaving networks of affection, memory, and resistance. We embroidered while the men waged war. And in embroidery, we told our version of history.

Free women are dangerous for a society that needs submissive women. That is why Hecate became a “sorceress.” That is why Lilith became a “demon.” That is why Pombagira is still feared. That is why embroidery, for centuries, was treated as “a woman’s thing” read: “a lesser thing.”

But the needle, like the tongue, cuts.

Today, when I hold my embroidery hoop, I think of all of them: Hecate, Lilith, Pombagira, Rosana, Romero, Chimamanda, Rozsika, and all the witches burned at the stake. Women who did not bow. Women who used what they had, writing, the word, the needle to say: “here too is a place to create, to resist, to exist.”
